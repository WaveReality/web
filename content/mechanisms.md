A significant source of motivation for the [[pilot wave]] framework pursued here is derived from a **mechanistic**, "how could this possibly work" perspective on the quantum world. This is not a naive question at the level of an [[aether]] or some other kind of "mechanical" substrate for quantum phenomena, although certainly these considerations run the risk of being dismissed in one way or another. Instead, the goal is to understand how the physical structure and properties of things like EM waves, quantum wave functions, and discrete, localized particles might all interact with each other in plausible ways.

The point of departure is the pure-wave based picture that provides the foundation of the [[Standard Model]] description, which can be seen as providing a satisfying answer to certain critical such mechanistic questions, while leaving other aspects entirely unexplained. Hopefully this grounding in such a solid foundation provides some appreciation for the importance of the remaining gaps at this level of description.

Specifically, consider the [[gauge theory]] description of the minimal coupling of the electromagnetic (EM) field (i.e., [[Maxwell]'s equations) with a wave of charge described by the [[Dirac]] wave equation. This provides a complete and satisfying account for how long-range EM forces can interact with a charged particle like an electron, where the spatially-distributed nature of the Dirac wave can plausibly interact with the spatially-distributed EM wave to exert force.

Likewise, a distributed, conserved charge value can be computed from the Dirac wave, which can then provide a direct driving force onto the EM wave (i.e., the otherwise challenging and largely unresolved [[back-reaction]]). Thus, at a purely qualitative level, this purely wave-based interaction seems plausible: the physical properties of these waves are mutually compatible, and there is no obvious impediment for this system of interacting distributed waves to properly capture the basic physical phenomena of mutual force interactions.

However, this same property that makes waves so natural for communicating interacting forces also makes them bad at representing any kind of localized, unitary, conserved properties, because the wave mass continuously diffuses into an increasingly thin and widely distributed film. This is specifically a problem for the quantum wave associated with a particle like an electron, which is inevitably found to have a point-like discrete existence whenever it is measured. And, if the electron charge can remain overall localized, then it can continuously regenerate the appropriate Maxwell fields.

Furthermore, electrons can collide with other particles at high energies (in nature and in high-energy colliders), and end up transforming into other particles. The rules for these transformations involve strict forms of [[conservation]], where discrete properties like charge, spin, and [[lepton]]-vs-[[quark]] status are conserved in various ways. It is very difficult to understand how a widely-distributed Dirac wave could somehow be gathered back up to then be localized and appropriately converted into the relevant other particles. 

The conceptual problem with recovering a discrete, localized particle from a distributed quantum wave is indeed identical to the paradoxical and unexplained process invoked in the notion of measurement in the standard [[Copenhagen]] interpretation.

The solution to this problem provided by the pilot-wave approach is to introduce an always-localized, discrete [[particle]] that is somehow coupled to its corresponding quantum wave, and whose motion is guided by this wave, which is in turn influenced by the EM force fields. Because all measurements can be ultimately reduced to localization measurements of massive particles, the presence of this discrete particle can in-principle provide a reasonable explanation for why wave collapse is not needed for measurement, and for the high-energy particle transmutation phenomena.

There are several remaining puzzles that the basic pilot-wave framework does not explain, and which motivate the properties of the [[Spinfield Model]]:

* How does the discrete particle ensure that its corresponding quantum wave does not dissipate into oblivion, and that it remains sensibly coupled to the particle? This question of the particle-to-wave back-reaction was raised by de Broglie in his _double solution_ version of the pilot wave framework, but nobody has yet made a fully workable version of such a model that is consistent with standard quantum physics.

* Does the charge of the discrete particle directly drive the corresponding EM wave, or is it driven by a more distributed charge field that emerges out of the quantum wave, as in the waves-only picture? The argument in favor of the latter is that it can easily avoid the problem of very high field strengths in the immediate neighborhood of a discrete particle. Furthermore, in the [[cellular automaton]] framework, the discrete particle needs to make discrete jumps to a neighboring cell when it moves, and such jumps would create very high-amplitude, high-frequency disturbances in the force field generation.

* This latter problem is also a difficulty for the coupling of the particle back onto the quantum wave field. Thus, some kind of mechanism for managing this problem is necessary within the overall scope of the quantum back-reaction mechanism. One possibility is that the unique properties of the [[Weyl]] wave function, which is required for the [[weak]] force system (i.e., the electroweak sector of the Standard Model), may provide a suitable affordance for discrete particle coupling.

In any case, aside from these remaining issues, the natural conclusion from the above mechanistic considerations is that waves should be used for forces (i.e., [[boson]]s) and discrete particles coupled with quantum waves (i.e., the pilot-wave model) for massive particles ([[fermions]]). The retention of classical force fields is the essential feature of the [[semiclassical]] framework. 

The semiclassical approach can be more strongly motivated by thinking in greater detail about how an alternative, particle-based ontology would work in the context of force interactions. If you just have a simple discrete point-like particle sitting in some kind of lattice-like grid, it is very difficult to implement realistic force-field interactions among such particles, especially when using other discrete particles like [[photon]]s to mediate these interactions. Do we really imagine that an electron is constantly spewing baseball-like photons out in all directions to hit other electrons? How does such a scheme ever achieve any kind of smooth field-like coverage of space using discrete point-like entities? How many balls per femtosecond does it have to spew? How do they manage to spread out uniformly over space and time, while properly conveying the dynamic interactions among the magnetic and electric aspects of the wave functions?

As discussed in the [[photon]] page, the way in which a photon is actually represented in standard quantum [[field theory]] is purely in terms of its energy and frequency properties, with absolutely no localization properties at all. Thus, the photon of this framework is quite unlike any kind of discrete particle that we think of for the particle aspect of the pilot-wave framework. Indeed, the same holds for the way that fermion particles are represented in the standard field theory framework as well. But whereas it does make physical sense to introduce discrete localized particles for massive fermion particles in a pilot-wave framework, it really doesn't make sense to also do this for force-fields.

The implication of this is that the photon as a particle-like concept does not exist, even though a localized wave-packet type of EM wave is perfectly reasonable.
