The **Feynman diagram**, named for Richard Feynman who introduced them in 1948, provides a graphical representation of the nature of [[particle]] interactions in the [[Standard Model]]. Although the implication of each such diagram is to add terms into a cumulative [[Lagrangian]] describing the full interaction picture, and these Lagrangian terms are all defined in terms of wave functions, there are specific **nonlinear** rules implied by these localized interactions that capture the fundamental particle-like nature of quantum physics. The nonlinearity of these interactions is implied by the minimum energy thresholds required for an interaction to take place, and various other relevant conservation laws such as the conservation of [[lepton]] number.

The nonlinearity of these particle interactions stands in contrast to the purely linear nature of the abstract [[Hilbert space]] and [[Schrodinger]] wave functions, which therefore require the exponentially-explosive [[configuration space]] to capture any nonlinear interactions. Thus, the major [[interpretations]] of quantum physics, which are all based on this purely linear, configuration-space picture, are failing to take into account the nonlinearity of particle interactions that is implied in each of the Feynman diagrams.

A major hypothesis behind the [[Spinfield Model]] is that by actually having a mechanism driving the nonlinear particle interactions captured in Feynman diagrams, one can finally do away with configuration space, and obtain a [[tools-vs-models|physical model]] that operates in regular 3D space, as in the [[cellular-automaton]] framework.

[[#figure_feynman]] shows the elementary force interactions in the Standard Model, using Feynman diagrams. Although these diagrams look like actual spatial trajectories of particles moving through 3D space, that is very much _not_ the case. Instead, each such diagram corresponds to factors that must be added to an underlying [[path integral]] formulation, which is then computed in the [[field theory]] framework of Fourier (Fock) space, which only represents energy / momenta, and has no spatial information at all.

Critically, the path-integral framework computes _all possible_ ways in which some kind of particle interaction could possibly take place, including different spatial locations and other such parameters, with the predicted results being _probabilities_ of observing the corresponding specific outcomes across many different repeated trials of a specific kind of particle interaction. In this way, the overall framework retains the core [[Copenhagen]] probabilistic / epistemic approach, and the infinities and other complexities resulting from the path integral framework (see [[renormalization]]) have generally obscured the interpretation of Feynman diagrams as reflecting discrete, nonlinear particle interactions.

By dealing only in momentum / energy space, the field theory framework massively simplifies the problem relative to having to explicitly deal with computing probabilities over different spatial trajectories. It is this joint calculation across these complementary basis representations, with the localization remaining implicit in the interactions that are computed, that enables the framework to capture the relevant phenomena.

Nevertheless, the underlying Standard Model theory holds that all fundamental (non-composite) [[fermion]] particles such as an [[electron]] or a [[quark]] are point particles, and that their interaction is mediated by the EM field as described by [[Maxwell]]'s equations, which falls off as a function of $1/r^2$ in force magnitude. Thus, these spatial localization properties are built into the nature of the particle interactions.

Furthermore, the data against which the theory is compared is based on tracks of particles in detector chambers, which inevitably show [[particle|discrete particle trajectories]] consistent with particles discretely colliding and decaying into other particles at entirely specific, localized points within the overall trajectory. Although a purely wave-based, configuration-space framework can be used to compute such trajectories ([[@Mott29]]; [[@FigariTeta13]]), the [[pilot-wave]] model provides an explicit physical model that directly aligns with this observed phenomenology.

There has been no significant attempt to capture the complexity of the actual Standard Model within a pilot wave framework, and the fact that particles can decay into other particles has not been integrated at all. The [[Spinfield Model]] at least provides a conceptual framework in which all of this phenomenology could occur, along with a [[stochastic motion]] mechanism that could account for the probabilistic nature of the quantum behavior.
