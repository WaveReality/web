The concept of a **photon** as an individual quantum of electromagnetic (EM) radiation was introduced (although not named as such) by [[@^Einstein05b]], in his description of the **photoelectric effect**, where the _frequency_ of the EM radiation is the critical variable in determining its energy, not the amplitude as in a purely classical field. This is described by the [[Planck relation]], and provided an early building block of quantum physics (see [[history]]).

The use of the term [[particle]] to describe the photon raises all manner of difficult questions, and is largely at odds with how the photon is actually treated in the [[Standard Model]] ([[@Klassen11]]; [[@Lamb95]]; [[@GerryKnight05]]). Specifically, the [[field theory]] second quantization representation of a photon only involves a representation of its frequency-based energy, with no representation at all of any discrete position.

Given that photons would have wavelengths at macroscopic length scales, it is very difficult to imagine how any kind of discrete localization would be possible, and indeed this is the case ([[@NewtonWigner49]]; [[@Knight61]]; [[@Wightman62]]; [[@Amrein69]]; [[@Hegerfeldt98]]; [[@Keller05]]; [[@Bialynicki-BirulaBialynicka-Birula09]]; [[@Bialynicki-BirulaBialynicka-Birula12]]; [[@GullaRyenSkaar23]]).

Furthermore, the status of a standard quantum wave function for the photon (analogous to that for the massive [[fermion]] particles) is controversial ([[@Bialynicki-Birula94a]]; [[@Sipe95]]; [[@Bialynicki-Birula96]]; [[@HodgsonSouthallPurdyEtAl22]]). The main proposal is to construct a [[Weyl]]-like complex version of the classical [[Maxwell]] wave equations, which allows one to compute an energy density operator (just as we do with [[complex KG]] in comparison to the non-complex [[Klein-Gordon]] equation; see [[complex waves]] for further discussion).

However, this energy density is not normalized, and thus cannot be used as a probability function in the standard Born-rule [[Copenhagen]] framework, unless it is "manually" normalized by simply dividing by an integral over all of space. And it is unclear to what extent it generates novel predictions relative to the classical [[Maxwell]] equations.

In addition, [[@^PalmerducaQin25]] show that it is impossible to derive the spin angular momentum, separate from orbital angular momentum, from massless bosons such as the photon and the graviton. The assertion that the photon has spin 1 derives from the two polarization directions of light. It is nothing like the quantum spin associated with massive particles, and is instead really just a relabeling of properties already known in Maxwell's equations ([[@BliokhAlonsoOstrovskayaEtAl10]]; [[@BliokhSmirnovaNori15]]).

See the [[semiclassical]] approach for an alternative interpretation that retains the classical field representation of the EM force, interacting with quantum fermion particles.

## Quantum optics

There is a large and complex literature on quantum optics, of great relevance for the [[Bell]] inequality tests of [[non-locality]], which have often employed entangled "photons". A future update will include a discussion of this literature (e.g., [[@Keller05]]; [[@ForbesdeOliveiraDennis21]]; [[@RaymerLandesMarcus21]]; [[@BernardisMercurioLiberato24]]).

Of particular interest are the _photon statistics_ issues, such as the photon _bunching_ and _antibunching_ phenomena ([[@KimbleDagenaisMandel77]]; [[@HongOuMandel87]]; [[@TeichSaleh88]]; [[@MarshallSantos88]]; [[@Khrennikov11a]]).
