This page provides a summary of what remains as unresolved **challenges** in the quest to actually make sense of quantum physics, relative to the [[mechanisms]] conclusions and specific proposals of the [[Spinfield Model]].

## Non-locality

[[Non-locality]] and the [[Bell]] inequality tests, along with the associated exponential [[configuration space]], represent the primary unresolved challenge at the level of abstract quantum mechanics. Critically, this should be considered a challenge for the [[Standard Model]] as well, because the tensor-product configuration space state that is necessary to compute the predictions in these experiments is clearly in conflict with the constraints of [[special relativity]], even if the resulting predictions themselves end up being invariant to this underlying conflict. 

Given the fundamentally inexplicable nature of these issues in any current framework, it seems premature to hold up making progress on other fronts on this account. Thus, we can just put this on the stack, and come back to it once more tractable issues have been resolved. 

## Field-theory vs. classical waves

The Standard Model uses [[field theory]] and [[virtual particles]] (via [[Feynman diagrams]]) to compute many aspects of the theory, whereas the [[semiclassical]] approach suggests that at least the force-field waves remain as such, rather than taking on a particle-like status as treated in the field-theory formalism (which, as emphasized throughout, does not actually involve any actual discrete particles).

At least some of the roles of virtual particles in the standard framework are otherwise accounted for by the classical interacting field model, for example a virtual particle is used in the perturbative framework to model the basic interaction between the electron and the electromagnetic field, including the self-interaction effects.

Other effects require extensive sampling of initial configurations to compute expected values of outcomes -- this is something that can become computationally expensive in the classical wave model, but does not reflect a fundamental difference in underlying physics. 

One critical contribution of virtual particles is in representing the quantum vacuum state, which is teeming with random possibilities. It is possible that the quantum waves end up leaking out and creating a more active vacuum state expectation. The [[zero point]] framework shows how various quantum phenomena can potentially be explained in terms of a semiclassical system interacting with a specific form of vacuum state.

As discussed in [[vacuum]], and of relevance to the [[Higgs]] mechanism, the energy associated with the zero point field or the vacuum expectation of the Higgs exceeds cosmologically plausible values by so many orders of magnitude that they are considered "the worst theoretical predictions in the history of physics" [[@^HobsonEfstathiouLasenby06]]. Thus, there are a number of currently unresolved issues regarding the nature of this vacuum state, but various empirically-validated phenomena depend on its effects as represented in the Standard Model.
