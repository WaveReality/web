**Wave reality** is dedicated to exploring the idea that the **quantum wave function** is _real_, and not just a description of our state of [[epistemic vs ontic|epistemological]] ignorance. The reality of the wave function is strongly indicated by the classic [[double-slit]] experiment results, where some kind of spatially-distributed wave-like interference phenomenon seems to be influencing the trajectories of discrete particles. In addition, there are increasingly strong theoretical and empirical attempts to show that a purely epistemic account contradicts quantum theory ([[@PuseyBarrettRudolph12]], [[@RingbauerDuffusBranciardEtAl15]]).

The relationship between waves and [[particle]]s is the central conundrum in quantum physics, and there have been attempts to resolve these issues by somehow eliminating either the particles or the waves. For example, if you carefully examine the mathematical foundations of quantum physics, you find that there are only waves (or wave-like fields), suggesting that particles can be somehow entirely eliminated ([[@Hobson13]]; [[@Sebens22]]).

However, this idea doesn't take into account the way that [[Feynman diagram]]s introduce discrete particles into the overall mathematical framework of the [[Standard Model]]. These diagrams discretely enumerate _localized_ particle "scattering" interactions, and are an essential ingredient in how the quantum wave equations are actually used to compute empirical predictions, which then account for the many particle-like phenomena that have been reliably established. Furthermore, at a more abstract level, discrete particles are implicit in the _measurement process_ of standard quantum frameworks.

Thus, even though the raw ingredients of quantum theory are wave equations, they are actually _used_ in a way that ultimately describes the behavior of particles. The opposite view, that there are only particles, is even less tenable: as argued above, there are many strong indications that wave phenomena are required (see also [[@Hobson13]]).

The [[pilot-wave]] framework of de Broglie and Bohm ([[@Bohm52]]; [[@Norsen22a]]) instead embraces the **wave-particle** [[duality]] fully, by positing a physically real [[quantum wave]]s that influence the motion of discrete particles, which always have a well-defined location in 3D space. This framework naturally and intuitively explains many of the otherwise paradoxical phenomena in quantum physics, and it thus provides the foundation for the work here. We furthermore show that interactions between distributed waves and discrete particles provides "practical" solutions to "problems" that physics needs to "solve" (scare quotes indicate a recognition of the anthropomorphic perspective here), rather than being some kind of paradoxical bug that we just have to somehow learn to swallow.

## Organization

There are three major threads of content, organized under the following categories.

### Standard Model

[[Standard Model]] provides an in-depth description of the currently-accepted understanding of fundamental physics, which is made particularly concrete and accessible through the use of computational [[Simulations]] with intuitive, dynamic graphical renderings of the various kinds of [[wave]] functions that provide the foundation for this model, including the [[Klein-Gordon]], [[Maxwell]], and [[Dirac]] equations. As a non-physicist author, the presentation leans heavily into developing an intuitive conceptual-level understanding, with most of the math supported by the computational models that bring the equations to life.

This project is fully committed to **accounting for all the empirical data** that so strongly supports the Standard Model. There is no point in coming up with an elegant theory that is obviously false. We cannot impose our own aesthetic preferences onto Nature, and must humbly accept any irrefutable facts that have been reliably empirically established. However, there remains a huge underexplored space of possible physical mechanisms that could potentially account for all the data, and at least one can learn a lot by trying, no matter how daunting that might sound!

### Interpretations

[[Interpretations]] provides a discussion of various alternative attempts to understand the quantum realm. The most important point here is to clearly recognize the distinction between **calculational tools** versus **physical models** ([[tools vs models]]). Although this distinction is recognized by some people in some domains, it still seems that many of the fundamental confusions that persist are based on trying to interpret the potential physical reality of something that could otherwise be seen as a purely calculational tool. 

There are typically many different ways to calculate a prediction of an experimental result, but some of them involve what seem like obvious mathematical shortcuts or simplifications, for example the explicit use of $1/r^2$ in the Coulomb electric force law or Newton's theory of gravity. This would imply some kind of _action at a distance_, which indeed was a major source of consternation in the case of Newton's law. However, there are well-established alternatives to each of these laws that avoid this action at a distance formulation, while describing the exact same underlying physics. Clearly, any attempt to provide a physical interpretation of what Nature is actually doing should be focused on these more plausible alternatives.
    
The challenge in applying this simple-sounding logic to the quantum realm is of course that we do not yet have a physically plausible version of the Standard Model that accounts for all the relevant data. So how do we sort through all of the different possible formulations in search of such a model, and what kinds of hard constraints can we extract from the existing formulations? Exploring that space is the main goal of this set of content.
    
As a starting point, it is essential to recognize the differences between the most abstract, "old" formulation of quantum mechanics in terms of the [[Hilbert space]] evolving in a unitary fashion according to the [[Schrodinger]] wave function, and the actual complexities of the Standard Model. Most interpretations are restricted to the abstract formulation, which must be seen as a highly-simplified calculational tool even in relation to the known properties of the Standard Model.
    
The approach taken is guided by an attempt to build bridges between two opposing levels of analysis. From the top-down, the two thorniest, interrelated issues are the [[non-locality]] observed between entangled particles, and the exponentially-large [[configuration space]] needed to represent these non-local correlations in the abstract models. From the bottom-up, we build up from the [[cellular automaton]] (CA) framework, which is arguably the simplest way that physics could autonomously emerge in parallel, everywhere in the universe, all at once.

Is there any way to reconcile the seemingly-incompatible constraints across these two levels? The approach here is to adopt a pragmatic kind of "triage": exponential explosion and non-locality are _such_ intractable barriers to any kind of physical model within the CA framework, that, by comparison, having _some_ superluminal elements in the system is relatively much more tractable. In addition, fundamentally stochastic, non-deterministic behavior is also a relatively easy pill to swallow in comparison.

Have these levers, within the broader context of the pilot-wave framework where potentially superluminal quantum waves are constantly interconnecting discrete particles and driving interference and amplification between them, been sufficiently explored and ruled out? Are there other indications that the configuration space tool is fundamentally inaccurate from a physical model perspective?

### Spinfield Model

The [[Spinfield Model]] is the work-in-progress attempt to develop a fully explicit functional computational-level model of the phenomenology of the Standard Model, within the CA and pilot-wave framework. This model captures particle-like properties using a field of complex-valued [[harmonic oscillator]]s (CVHOs) that [[spin]], which interact bidirectionally with [[quantum wave]]s, such as the [[Dirac]] wave equations for an [[electron]], and via the electromagnetic field as described by the [[Maxwell]] wave functions. The particles move via [[stochastic motion]] driven by velocity values encoded in the phase relationships among the CVHOs.

Beyond all the basic quantum phenomena, the nature of [[neutrino]]s and the [[weak]] interactions provide strong constraints and inspiration for the Spinfield Model. For example, the _muon_ is a heavy [[generation]] of an electron, which decays into an electron and two neutrinos, via the weak force. This suggests that the extra mass / energy of the muon is some kind of excited state relative to an electron, and that it can be released in the form of these neutrinos.

There are specific constraints on the conservation of [[lepton]] number and generation, which determine that this extra energy is not released in the form of photons or other particles, further suggesting that the extra mass is somehow more directly tied to the properties of the neutrinos themselves. Neutrinos are the simplest (yet most mysterious) type of particle, consisting esentially of pure [[spin]]. Thus, they are a prime target of the Spinfield Model.
