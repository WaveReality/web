A **particle** is identified functionally as something having fundamental properties at least including a **rest mass**. According to the [[Standard Model]], all massive particles are [[fermion]]s, having [[spin]] 1/2. The rest mass of a particle is derived from the [[Higgs]] field.

The Standard Model also describes the [[boson]]s as particles (with [[spin]] 1) that carry forces to mediate interactions among massive particles. This is not included in the present [[Spinfield Model]], which instead adopts a [[semiclassical]] approach where forces are continuous-valued waves, with no corresponding particle-like properties. In this model, all particle-like effects attributed to e.g., electromagnetic forces instead result from the ways that these forces interact with fermion particles.

This approach is consistent with the lack of any kind of conservation laws or other constraints on boson numbers, and the lack of a proper quantum wave function describing a [[photon]]. Indeed, even the spin 1 of the photon is not well defined, in the same way that it is for fermions -- it is just the polarization of the EM field.

At one level of analysis, there doesn't appear to be any explicit representation of an actual discrete particle within the mathematical framework of the Standard Model, leading some to conclude that **particles don't exist in the Standard Model** ([[@Hobson13]]; [[@Sebens22]]). Instead, all of the equations in the Standard Model are various forms of [[wave]] equations, interacting via [[gauge theory]] [[Lagrangian]]s, which are then represented in the Fourier space of the [[field theory]] framework to compute relevant empirical predictions.

Furthermore, a particle in quantum field theory is represented entirely in _momentum space_ in terms of its energy, by adding or subtracting a single frequency mode to the overall Fourier-space field. As such, it has no localization, in contradiction to what one would typically image for a discrete particle-like entity.

However, it seems clear that discrete particles are explicitly represented in the [[Standard Model#Feynman diagrams]] that drive the path-integral framework used to actually perform computations in the Standard Model. Thus, it is actually the interplay between these discrete, localized particle-based interactions depicted in these diagrams, and the wave-based nature of the underlying fields, that gives rise to the predictions made from the Standard Model. Thus, contrary to the above-cited interpretations, it seems that the wave-particle [[duality]] is in fact alive and well in this model.

Nevertheless, the actual nature of these hypothesized particles remains rather different from what most people likely intuitively imagine. And the hugely complex nature of the Standard Model formalism does not really help in clarifying exactly what is going on. Therefore, it remains possible that force-field interactions that are described in terms of [[virtual particles]] (bosons) in the Standard Model could actually be captured in terms of classical wave fields interacting with appropriately-defined discretized massive fermion particles, as in the [[semiclassical]] framework.

Furthermore, the massive weak force bosons, and the [[Higgs]] boson, are extremely short-lived, further suggesting that they are just transient fluctuations in the underlying field at a specific energy level consistent with the ascribed rest mass, which then gives rise to other longer-lived particles that can actually be directly measured.

A central property of the [[pilot wave]] framework and the [[Spinfield Model]] is that the massive fermion particles actually do exist as distinct from their associated [[quantum wave]] functions, and that they remain localized to a specific, discrete location at all times.
